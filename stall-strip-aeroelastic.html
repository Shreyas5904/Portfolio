Aeroelastic Effects on Stall Strip Performance

Duke Aeroelasticity Lab Advisor: Earl Dowell
Project In Progress
  Builds on: Stall Strip Aerodynamics Investigation

Motivation

The stall strip wind tunnel investigation established clear aerodynamic baselines for a NACA 0012 airfoil fitted with a triangular leading-edge stall strip: controlled, gradual stall progression and significant post-stall lift retention compared to a clean airfoil.

That constraint is appropriate for isolating aerodynamic behavior, but it creates a gap between the experimental model and physical reality. Real wings are flexible structures that deflect and twist under aerodynamic load. The structural compliance couples with aerodynamic forces, creating feedback loops that can amplify response, trigger instability, or fundamentally alter flow behavior.

Stall strips are particularly sensitive to this. Their aerodynamic function depends critically on chordwise position relative to the stagnation point and interaction with the developing boundary layer. A geometric shift of even 1–2% chord can move the reattachment point or alter leading-edge suction recovery—both central to stall strip performance.

Core question: When a wing fitted with a stall strip undergoes aeroelastic deformation — bending, twisting, or flutter-driven oscillation — does the stall strip retain its designed aerodynamic benefit, or do aeroelastic effects reduce its effectiveness or introduce unintended aerodynamic phenomena?

Aeroelastic Phenomena Under Investigation

Three distinct coupling mechanisms between structural deformation and stall strip aerodynamics are being investigated, each operating through a different physical pathway.

Static Aeroelastic Twist

Under steady aerodynamic loading, a flexible wing twists about its elastic axis, changing local angle of attack spanwise. The stall strip's effectiveness depends on how closely it remains aligned with the developing flow's pressure gradient. Elastic twist can misalign the strip relative to flow, reducing reattachment control.

Flutter

Flutter is an aeroelastic instability in which energy is continuously extracted from the flow into coupled pitch-plunge oscillation. A stall strip on a fluttering wing experiences transient angle-of-attack excursions, triggering dynamic stall vortices that may feed energy back into the structure and destabilize the system further.

Limit Cycle Oscillation

Beyond the flutter boundary, many structures settle into bounded periodic oscillations stabilized by aerodynamic or structural nonlinearity. The stall strip introduces a delayed stall response; the hypothesis is that this smooth aerodynamic curve may lower the amplitude envelope that bounds the oscillation.

Dynamic Stall

When a wing oscillates through stall, aerodynamic response lags the motion, producing hysteresis in lift and moment curves with peak loads exceeding quasi-steady predictions. The stall strip may shift the onset angle and alter the vortex shedding dynamics, changing both transient loads and flutter stability.

Design safety implication: A wing designed to a flutter margin using clean-airfoil aerodynamics may have a different flutter speed once a stall strip is installed. This coupling must be characterized to avoid over-predicting stability margins.

Hypotheses

Based on the stall strip aerodynamic results and classical aeroelastic theory, the following predictions drive the experimental design.

H1 — Divergence Speed

Divergence speed scales inversely with the effective lift curve slope CLα. The stall strip reduces CL,max and softens the stall break but has a small effect on the pre-stall slope. Therefore, divergence speed is expected to change only slightly with the stall strip installation.

H2 — Flutter Speed

The stall strip shifts the effective stall angle earlier, meaning the wing enters nonlinear aerodynamic behavior at lower dynamic pressure. The hypothesis is that leading-edge placement at 0% chord will defer the flutter transition to higher speeds by maintaining attached flow longer, while higher placements will have less beneficial effect.

H3 — Limit Cycle Oscillation

The gradual post-stall lift curve from the stall strip removes a sharp aerodynamic nonlinearity that would otherwise act as a restoring force to bound LCO amplitude. The hypothesis is that LCO amplitude will increase with the stall strip, particularly for 0% chord placement.

H4 — Dynamic Stall

The stall strip is expected to shift dynamic stall onset to a lower angle of attack, consistent with its static behavior, and to reduce the peak suction driving the dynamic stall vortex growth, thereby lowering transient loads during flutter or forced oscillation.

Plan of Action

1

Analytical Baseline — Typical Section Model

Implement the 2-DOF pitch-plunge typical section equations of motion in MATLAB, substituting measured stall strip lift curves as aerodynamic inputs in place of the standard thin-airfoil approximation. This provides an a priori prediction of flutter speed, divergence speed, and LCO behavior.

2

Elastic Rig Design and Fabrication

Modify the existing wind tunnel mounting system to introduce controlled structural compliance. The rigid stainless steel rod is replaced with a calibrated spring system — specifically, a cantilevered aluminum spar with stiffness tuned to yield a first natural frequency of approximately 8–12 Hz.

3

Instrumentation

Add strain gauges at the root to measure bending moment and torque as a function of airspeed, and accelerometers at the tip to capture oscillation onset, frequency, and amplitude. A high-speed camera will record qualitative flutter behavior and vortex shedding.

4

Wind Tunnel Test Campaign

Sweep freestream velocity from well below to just above the predicted flutter boundary for four configurations: clean elastic wing (baseline), strip at 0%, 1.5%, and 3% chord. Record flutter speed, limit cycle amplitude, and post-flutter divergence behavior for each.

5

Dynamic Stall Characterization

At a fixed velocity near 80% of VF, impose forced pitching oscillations across a range of frequencies and amplitudes that drive the section through stall. Record instantaneous lift, moment, and lift coefficient hysteresis loops. Compare dynamic stall behavior across stall strip configurations.

6

Analysis and Comparison Against Hypotheses

Correlate measured flutter speeds, divergence speeds, and LCO amplitudes against the analytical predictions from Step 1. Identify where the typical section model with modified aerodynamics successfully predicts behavior and where nonlinear or 3D effects introduce departures. Formulate design guidelines for stall strip placement on flexible wings.
